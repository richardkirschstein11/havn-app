# Sleep environment engineering for a prefabricated room-in-room sleeping cabin (2.0–2.4 × 2.4 × 2.2 m, Berlin pilot Nov 2026)

Research-method note: two parallel web-search batches were completed (≈26 queries). The shared web-search budget was then exhausted and the sandbox's egress proxy refused every direct fetch of primary sources (WHO, PubMed/PMC, IWBI/WELL, REHVA, DTU Orbit, Framery, Northwestern). Consequently many figures below rest on search snippets and secondary summaries rather than on the full papers; each such case is flagged. Items that could not be sourced at all are listed under Gaps rather than asserted. Evidence older than 2018 is marked "(older)".

## Key Question 1 — Which concrete target values (dB, °C, %RH, CO2 ppm, air changes, lux) define a best-in-class sleep cabin, and which standards back them?

### Takeaway
A defensible "best-in-class" specification is: ≤30 dB(A) LAeq inside the cabin at night (WHO), wall/door assembly on par with or better than DIN 4109's hotel-room requirement (R'w 47 dB normal / 52 dB enhanced walls, 32/37 dB corridor doors) rather than the ~30 dB speech-level reduction typical of office pods; 20–25 °C room air (best evidence, older adults) with a cooler 16–20 °C option and bed-level temperature control; CO2 held below ~800 ppm absolute (EN 16798-1 Category I ≈ 10 l/s = 36 m³/h per person) and never above 1,000 ppm; and near-total darkness, since one night at ~100 lux measurably raises heart rate and next-morning insulin resistance.

### Cited Findings

#### (a) Acoustics — targets and standards
- WHO/Europe's noise fact sheet states that the WHO community-noise guidelines recommend less than 30 dB(A) in bedrooms during the night; the fact sheet is dated 2011 and the 30 dB(A) value predates the 2018 guideline update (older, 1999 community-noise guidelines) — [WHO/Europe noise fact sheet](https://www.who.int/Europe/news-room/fact-sheets/item/noise)
- The 2018 WHO Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region give source-specific night values at the façade (outdoor): 45 dB Lnight for road traffic, 44 dB Lnight for rail, 40 dB Lnight for aircraft (the lowest of the five sources) — [RIVM summary of WHO advice values](https://www.rivm.nl/ggd-richtlijn-mmk-omgevingsgeluid/gezondheidseffecten-geluid/advieswaarden-who); [FHI (Norway) summary of the 2018 guidelines](https://www.fhi.no/ml/miljo/stoy/nye-retningslinjer-for-stoy-fra-verdens-helseorganisasjon/); [UECNA on 45 dB Lden / 40 dB Lnight for aircraft](https://uecna.eu/who-45-db-lden-and-40-db-lnight-are-max/)
- DIN 4109 (Germany): a practitioner thread cites DIN 4109 requiring R'w = 47 dB for walls between guest rooms in Beherbergungsstätten (normal requirement) with the corridor door at Rw = 32 dB, and DIN 4109 Beiblatt 2 (enhanced) requiring R'w = 52 dB wall with Rw = 37 dB door; the thread notes the door can drag the combined wall+door value below target and that a combined check is required (forum source, verify against DIN 4109-1:2018) — [bau.de forum: Schallschutz Hotelzimmer](https://bau.de/forum/bauphysik/10212.php)
- Door manufacturers' handbooks list "Türen zwischen Fluren und Übernachtungsräumen 32 dB (SK2), ≥ 37 dB (SK3 enhanced)" — [Garant Türenhandbuch](https://tuerenhandbuch.garant.de/typo3conf/ext/cdt_cmos2/Resources/Public/cmos2/php/get.php?id=5724); [PRÜM Schallschutz handbook 2024 (PDF)](https://www.fries24.de/download/pruem_t_ekb_09_2024_schall.pdf)
- WELL v2 S02 (Maximum Noise Levels) sets average and maximum sound-pressure-level thresholds in dBA and dBC for three space categories and allows a 20 dB gap between average and maximum levels; an acoustics commentary argues S02 is too permissive of low-frequency noise and recommends lowering C-weighted limits by a further 5 dB (exact S02 dBA values were not retrievable — see Gaps) — [Springer Nature community post on WELL S02 and LFN](https://communities.springernature.com/posts/are-well-building-standard-s02-sound-levels-too-permissive-of-low-frequency-noise-addressing-the-lfn-in-the-room)

#### (a) Acoustics — what room-in-room pods actually achieve
- Office pods are rated under ISO 23351-1 by speech level reduction D_S,A, not by Rw; Framery states its pods reach D_S,A > 30 dB — [Framery: soundproof insulation](https://framery.com/en/soundproof-insulation/)
- A Turku University of Applied Sciences test of Framery O against ten other best-selling booths under ISO 23351-1 reported 30.3 dB speech level reduction (vendor-publicised study) — [Framery press release](https://framery.com/en/framery-proven-to-be-best-in-sound-insulation-according-to-new-industry-standard/)
- A competitor pod is reported at 30.9 dB Class A speech reduction tested by SGS under ISO 23351-1; ISO 23351-1 was introduced because prior ratings (e.g. NIC) were not comparable — [ecommercefastlane: top office pod brands](https://ecommercefastlane.com/top-office-pod-brands-in-the-usa/)

#### (b) Thermal — targets
- Baniassadi et al. 2023, "Nighttime ambient temperature and sleep in community-dwelling older adults", Science of the Total Environment: ~50 adults aged ≥65, ~11,000 person-nights with wearables and bedroom temperature sensors; sleep efficiency was best between 68–77 °F (≈20–25 °C) and fell 5–10 % as temperature rose from 25 °C to 30 °C; efficiency also fell below 20 °C; large inter-individual variation (Celsius values are conversions by secondary outlets; original paper not accessed) — [WebMD](https://www.webmd.com/sleep-disorders/news/20230915/older-adults-best-sleep-temp-study); [The Week](https://www.theweek.in/news/health/2023/08/29/room-temp-of-20-25-degrees-celsius-linked-to-better-sleep-in-the.amp.html)
- General guidance from sleep clinicians is cooler: 60–68 °F (15.6–20 °C) per Sleep Foundation and 60–67 °F (15.6–19.4 °C) per a Cleveland Clinic specialist — [Sleep Foundation: bedroom temperature](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/bedroom-environment/bedroom-temperature); [The Healthy / Cleveland Clinic](https://thehealthy.com/sleep/this-is-the-best-temperature-for-great-sleep-says-a-cleveland-clinic-expert)
- A 2023 Journal of Thermal Biology study on quilt warmth found that with a thin duvet the optimal room-temperature range is narrow, whereas heavier bedding lets people sleep comfortably across a much wider room-temperature span — [Sleep Foundation news summary](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-news/bedroom-temperatures-and-bedding-choices-affect-sleep)

#### (c) Air quality — ventilation standards
- EN 16798-1 per-person outdoor-air rates (non-adapted persons): Category I ≈ 10 l/s (36 m³/h), II ≈ 7 l/s (25 m³/h), III ≈ 4 l/s (14 m³/h), IV ≈ 2.5 l/s, with a floor that total ventilation must never be below 4 l/s per person; a building-emission component is added per m² (low-polluting building: 1.0 / 0.7 / 0.4 / 0.3 l/(s·m²) for Cat I–IV; Cat I is 0.5 / 1.0 / 2.0 l/(s·m²) for very-low / low / non-low-polluting buildings) — [REHVA Journal: European standardisation on IAQ (Olesen)](https://www.rehva.eu/rehva-journal/chapter/european-standardisation-on-indoor-air-quality); [Olesen AIVC slides](https://aivc.org/system/files/7-Bjarne%20Olesen.pdf)
- EN 16798-1 design CO2 above outdoor, assuming 20 l/(h·person) emission: Category I 550 ppm (10 l/s), II 800 ppm (7 l/s), III 1,350 ppm (4 l/s); the standard is under revision — [REHVA Journal (Olesen)](https://www.rehva.eu/rehva-journal/chapter/european-standardisation-on-indoor-air-quality); [AIVC 2026 workshop slides (Olesen)](https://www.aivc.org/system/files/7_Workshop%202026_Olesen.pdf)
- Eurovent's IAQ scheme: CO2 "recommended" < 900 ppm, "acceptable" 900–1,200 ppm, "poor" > 1,200 ppm; recommended outdoor airflow > 36 m³/h (10 l/s) per person — [Eurovent IAQ flyer 2024 (PDF)](https://www.eurovent.eu/wp-content/uploads/publications-files/2024-eurovent-iaq-flyer-web.pdf)
- A DTU review of bedroom ventilation concludes that absolute CO2 generated by sleeping occupants should as a minimum stay below 1,000 ppm and preferably below 800 ppm, and that bedroom outdoor-air supply rates should be at least doubled relative to existing standards (authors note more research needed) — [DTU Orbit: How much ventilation is necessary to avoid disturbance to sleep](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/how-much-ventilation-is-necessary-to-avoid-disturbance-to-sleep/)
- ASHRAE Journal column (Wargocki) proposes keeping bedroom CO2 below 800 ppm, stating the recommendation still requires validation — [ASHRAE Journal: Using IAQ tactics to sleep better](https://www.ashrae.org/news/ashraejournal/using-indoor-air-quality-tactics-to-sleep-better-at-night-perform-well-the-next-day)
- WELL v2: A03 Option 4 requires CO2 ≤ 900 ppm in occupiable spaces; A06 Enhanced Ventilation awards points for exceeding ASHRAE 62.1-2010 outdoor-air rates by 30 % or 60 %, or for demand-controlled ventilation holding CO2 below 900 ppm or 750 ppm (a three-tier 900/750/600 ppm table is cited by one project); A05 Enhanced Air Quality gives points for PM2.5 ≤ 10 µg/m³ against a basic 15 µg/m³ — [Kaiterra: IAQ requirements in WELL v2](https://learn.kaiterra.com/en/resources/indoor-air-quality-requirements-and-credits-in-well-v2-leed-v4-and-reset-air); [Phipps Conservatory WELL appendix](https://www.phipps.conservatory.org/incorporating-the-well-building-standard-across-the-conservatory/welcome-center/dive-wc/appendix-air-wc); [WELL AP study guide A03/A06/A08](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/well-ap/air-concept/ventilation-design-monitoring); [Atmotube on WELL monitoring](https://atmotube.com/blog/air-quality-monitoring-for-well-certification)

#### (f) Light — targets
- Northwestern (Zee, Grimaldi) PNAS, March 2022: one night of moderate ambient light during sleep versus a dim room increased heart rate during sleep (cardiovascular strain) and worsened insulin resistance the following morning — [Northwestern News](https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2022/03/close-the-blinds-during-sleep-to-protect-your-health); [Feinberg School news](https://news.feinberg.northwestern.edu/2022/03/14/exposure-to-artificial-light-during-sleep-may-increase-risk-of-heart-disease-and-diabetes/)
- A secondary (retailer) summary of that study gives the conditions as < 3 lux versus ~100 lux, n = 20 healthy adults, with a ~16 % drop in the Matsuda insulin-sensitivity index in the lit condition (unverified against the primary paper) — [Sleepout blog summary](https://sleepoutcurtains.com/blogs/blackout-experts/sleeping-with-the-light-on-what-it-does-to-your-heart)

### Inferences
- Proposed cabin target sheet (synthesis of the citations above; the cabin-specific numbers are my derivations):
  - Noise inside cabin: LAeq,night ≤ 30 dB(A) (WHO bedroom value); design the envelope to DIN 4109 Beiblatt 2 "enhanced" hotel values (R'w ≥ 52 dB wall equivalent, door ≥ 37 dB) because a 2.2 m-high panel box with a door and ventilation penetrations will be door- and flanking-limited. An office pod's D_S,A ≈ 30 dB implies that in a 60 dB(A) lobby the interior would sit near 30 dB(A) (just meeting WHO), but in a 70 dB(A) station concourse it would be ~40 dB(A), i.e. 10 dB above target — a station deployment needs roughly 10 dB more attenuation than a standard office pod, plus masking.
  - Ventilation: at 36 m³/h (EN 16798-1 Cat I) the 10.6 m³ (2.0×2.4×2.2) cabin sees ≈3.4 air changes per hour and the 12.7 m³ (2.4×2.4×2.2) cabin ≈2.8 ACH; a 180 m³/h unit at full speed is ≈14–17 ACH, so it can idle at ~20 % and boost on CO2. Using the standard's 20 l/h CO2 emission and 36 m³/h gives ≈550 ppm above outdoor (≈970 ppm absolute at 420 ppm outdoors); a sleeping person emits less than the sedentary design value, so the same 36 m³/h should land in the 750–850 ppm range — consistent with the "< 800 ppm" DTU/ASHRAE recommendation. Set CO2 boost at ~750 ppm and alarm at 1,000 ppm.
  - Temperature: offer a user-selectable room setpoint of 18–24 °C with a 20 °C default (inside the 20–25 °C Baniassadi band and at the top of the clinician 16–20 °C band), and pair it with a heavier duvet option because bedding widens the comfortable room-temperature range.
  - Light: < 1 lux at the pillow during sleep (the PNAS study's dim condition was < 3 lux); dimmable warm wake light.
  - Humidity: EN 16798-1 category RH ranges were not retrieved (see Gaps); the brief's 40–60 % target is consistent with WELL/EN practice but I could not cite a specific clause.

## Key Question 2 — Which interventions have the strongest evidence for better sleep (objective and subjective), and what are their effect sizes? Ranked.

### Takeaway
Ranked by strength of evidence and effect size for a self-service cabin: (1) darkness and (2) quiet are foundational (physiological harm from light at night is shown in a controlled study; WHO guideline values rest on large epidemiological bases); (3) keeping CO2 under ~800–1,000 ppm yields small but reproducible objective gains (+1–2 % sleep efficiency, −5–8 min awake, more deep sleep); (4) temperature in the 20–25 °C band is worth 5–10 % sleep efficiency versus an overheated room; (5) weighted blankets show a large effect in one 120-person RCT (≈60 % vs 5 % responders) but in a psychiatric-insomnia population; (6) lavender inhalation shows a moderate pooled effect (SMD ≈ −0.56) from low-quality trials; (7) broadband sound masking has the weakest evidence (low-GRADE, mostly subjective) but no reported adverse effects; cooling mattress pads have marketing claims and no RCT found here; ionisers are net-negative.

### Cited Findings

#### Air quality / CO2 (objective, small-to-moderate effects)
- Strøm-Tejsen et al. (DTU; Roomvent 2014 conference version, later Indoor Air 2016): 16 students in identical single dormitory rooms north of Copenhagen; a fan switched on automatically when CO2 exceeded 900 ppm; average CO2 was 2,395 ppm (low ventilation) vs 835 ppm (increased ventilation); higher ventilation improved rated air freshness, mental state and feeling rested, produced a significant positive effect on actigraphic sleep efficiency and on next-day performance, with slightly higher rated mouth and skin dryness — [ASHRAE Journal summary](https://www.ashrae.org/news/ashraejournal/using-indoor-air-quality-tactics-to-sleep-better-at-night-perform-well-the-next-day); [Roomvent 2014 paper (DTU PDF)](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/101895512/Roomvent_2014_Paper_ID_148.pdf); [DTU Orbit record: CO2-controlled bedroom ventilation](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/the-effect-of-cosub2sub-controlled-bedroom-ventilation-on-sleep-a/)
- The same group's earlier open- vs closed-window study (night CO2 660 vs 2,585 ppm) found no significant effect on the sleep-quality scale or next-day performance, though sleep latency and perceived freshness improved (older) — [ASHRAE Journal summary](https://www.ashrae.org/news/ashraejournal/using-indoor-air-quality-tactics-to-sleep-better-at-night-perform-well-the-next-day)
- Mishra et al., Indoor Air 2018 (online Nov 2017; DOI 10.1111/ina.12435): 17 healthy young adults, 5 nights, window or internal door open vs both closed; CO2 averaged ~717 ppm (SD 197) open vs ~1,150 ppm (SD 463) closed; lower CO2 was associated with better sleep depth, sleep efficiency and fewer awakenings, but only questionnaire sleep depth and actigraphy-based sleep phase reached significance; the author notes an open door was less effective than a window at lowering CO2 but gave similar sleep quality — [HU publication record](https://www.internationalhu.com/research/publications/windowdoor-openingmediated-bedroom-ventilation-and-its-impact-on-sleep-quality-of-healthy-young-adul); [Sci.News summary](https://www.sci.news/medicine/opening-windows-doors-sleep-quality-05459.html); [Google Store feature quoting Mishra](https://store.google.com/intl/en/ideas/articles/sleep-with-fresh-air)
- DTU field-lab (climate-chamber bedroom) study: 36 young adults slept at ventilation producing average CO2 of 750, 1,000 and 1,300 ppm; relative to 750 ppm, sleep efficiency fell 1.3 % and 1.8 % and time awake rose 5.0 and 7.8 min at 1,000 and 1,300 ppm; deep-sleep duration decreased at 1,300 ppm; authors recommend avoiding average bedroom CO2 ≥ 1,000 ppm (DTU record; the search did not confirm the author list — likely Fan/Wargocki group, Building and Environment) — [DTU Orbit: Ventilation causing an average CO2 of 1000 ppm negatively affects sleep](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/ventilation-causing-an-average-cosub2sub-concentration-of-1000-pp/)
- DTU single-blind field intervention (Science of the Total Environment, 2023 per the record): fan speed covertly changed to give low/medium/high ventilation in real bedrooms; in the 12 bedrooms where the conditions clearly differed, participants had less deep sleep and more awakenings at lower ventilation; no differences in cognitive performance — [DTU Orbit: single-blind field intervention](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/a-single-blind-field-intervention-study-of-whether-increased-bedr/)
- Danish field study in 38 bedrooms: air pollutants were not significantly correlated with any measured sleep parameter, but subjects rated sleep better with window or door open — [DTU Orbit: Bedroom ventilation field study Denmark](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/bedroom-ventilation-in-relation-to-sleep-quality-a-field-study-in/)
- A study of 22 older volunteers compared low vs high mechanical ventilation; outcome not visible in the snippet — [Gerontechnology journal record](https://journal.gerontechnology.org/currentIssueContent.aspx?aid=3288)

#### Thermal (objective, moderate effect)
- Baniassadi 2023: 5–10 % lower sleep efficiency when bedroom temperature rose from 25 °C to 30 °C; optimum 20–25 °C in older adults; efficiency also declined below 20 °C — [WebMD](https://www.webmd.com/sleep-disorders/news/20230915/older-adults-best-sleep-temp-study)
- Bedding thickness modulates the optimal room-temperature window (2023, Journal of Thermal Biology) — [Sleep Foundation news](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-news/bedroom-temperatures-and-bedding-choices-affect-sleep)

#### Light (objective physiological effect, single night)
- One night at moderate light (~100 lux per secondary source) vs dim (< 3 lux) raised sleeping heart rate and worsened next-morning insulin resistance in healthy adults (PNAS 2022) — [Northwestern News](https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2022/03/close-the-blinds-during-sleep-to-protect-your-health); [Sleepout summary (lux and n unverified)](https://sleepoutcurtains.com/blogs/blackout-experts/sleeping-with-the-light-on-what-it-does-to-your-heart)
- Zee recommends blackout shades or eye masks to avoid outdoor light (expert advice, not trial evidence) — [Northwestern News](https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2022/03/close-the-blinds-during-sleep-to-protect-your-health)

#### Weighted blankets (subjective ISI, large effect in a clinical population)
- Ekholm, Spulber & Adler, Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine 2020: 120 adults with insomnia plus a co-occurring psychiatric diagnosis (major depression, bipolar, GAD or ADHD), blindly randomised 1:1 to four weeks at home with a chain-weighted blanket vs a light control blanket; primary outcome Insomnia Severity Index — [Natural Medicine Journal review](https://naturalmedicinejournal.com/journal/weighted-blankets-better-sleep); [NHRI summary](https://naturalhealthresearch.org/weighted-chain-blankets-effective-intervention-for-insomnia-in-psychiatric-disorders/)
- Secondary coverage reports that nearly 60 % of weighted-blanket users responded (≥ 50 % ISI reduction) versus 5.4 % of controls (figures from news/blog summaries; primary paper not accessed) — [Onmanorama](https://onmanorama.com/lifestyle/health/2020/09/27/weighted-blankets-found-improve-sleep-insomnia-patients.html)
- Zhao et al. 2024, Complementary Therapies in Medicine 87:103104 — systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs of weighted blankets in patients with mental disorders; pooled effect sizes not visible in retrieved snippets — [PubMed 39447684](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39447684/); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/7e51197bf6044d0dad94114554aa5caa)
- Counter-evidence: a randomised crossover trial in 67 autistic children found no objective sleep improvement although children and parents preferred the blanket; a 2024 practice guideline for autistic children does not list weighted blankets as evidence-informed for sleep (commercial-blog source, verify) — [Acibadem blog](https://acibademinternational.com/blog/weighted-blankets-what-trials-show-for-sleep-and-anxiety-related-restlessness/)

#### Lavender / aromatherapy (subjective, moderate pooled effect, low-quality trials)
- 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of 11 RCTs, 628 adults: lavender essential oil improved sleep quality, SMD = −0.56 (95 % CI −0.96 to −0.17, p = 0.005); authors caution that the number and quality of studies limit the conclusion — [PMC12904233](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12904233)
- Another meta-analysis found larger efficacy with lavender as the sole aroma (SMD = −1.39, 95 % CI −2.06 to −0.72), with larger effects for non-inhaled routes and courses shorter than 4 weeks (population per search summary: older adults — attribution not verified) — [PMC11630975](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11630975/)
- In cancer patients, a 10-RCT meta-analysis found lavender had the best effect among single oils, SMD = −1.06 (95 % CI −1.49 to −0.63) — [BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies 2022 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/75a5f57f132547d580a45630abd350f9)
- A 2021 systematic review/meta-analysis in adults and elderly concluded aromatherapy improves sleep quality and reduces stress, pain, anxiety, depression and fatigue — [PubMed 34166869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34166869/)
- Menopausal women: 4-study meta-analysis, SMD 1.338 (95 % CI 0.329–2.347) favouring lavender (very few trials) — [TBSRJ meta-analysis](https://publish.kne-publishing.com/index.php/tbsrj/article/view/6175)
- A 2020 Korean-language systematic review/meta-analysis on aromatherapy and sleep exists but its abstract reports the effect size inconsistently — [PubMed 31932562](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31932562/)

#### Sound masking / broadband noise (weak evidence, no harms reported)
- Riedy et al. 2021 (Sleep Medicine Reviews, "Noise as a sleep aid"): 38 studies; inconsistent methods, small samples, mostly subjective outcomes; GRADE quality of evidence low — [McGill OSS summary](https://www.mcgill.ca/oss/node/11923)
- Capezuti et al., J Clin Sleep Med (online Dec 2021, print 2022): 34 studies, 1,103 persons; white noise 18 studies, pink noise 11, multi-audio 6; positive sleep findings in 6/18 white (33 %), 9/11 pink (≈82 %), 4/6 multi-audio (67 %); multi-audio studies had the lowest risk of bias; conclusion: no strong evidence to support auditory stimulation, but no adverse effects reported with short-term use — [PubMed 34964434](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34964434/); [PMC9163611](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9163611/)
- A later review cited in Sleep found 56 % of studies reported positive effects of broadband noise on sleep, largely on low-quality evidence — [Sleep (OUP) 2026 article](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/49/5/zsag032/8456219)
- Sleep Foundation overview of pink noise (consumer-level) — [Sleep Foundation: pink noise](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/pink-noise)

#### Ionisers (evidence of harm / no benefit)
- Alshawa et al. 2007 (older), Environ. Sci. Technol.: some ionisers emit ozone, which reacts with unsaturated VOCs such as limonene to nucleate ultrafine particles; observed in a ventilated office after adding D-limonene — [PDXScholar record](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/mengin_fac/344)
- Kim et al. 2017 review (Environmental Pollution): indoor ioniser use is limited by poor removal efficiency and production of noxious by-products such as ozone, NOx and VOC oxidation intermediates (older) — [science.gov topic page: air ionization](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/a/air+ionization)
- 2021 Building and Environment study of an in-duct bipolar ionisation unit: decreased some hydrocarbons (xylenes) but increased oxygenated VOCs (acetone, ethanol) and toluene; minimal impact on particles, O3 and NO2 — [science.gov topic page: air ionization](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/a/air+ionization); vendor summary with unverified "+800 % ultrafine" figures — [Smart Air blog](https://smartairfilters.com/blog/ionizers-can-increase-pollution-particles-formaldehyde/)

### Inferences
- Ranking for a cabin (objective-evidence weighted): 1. envelope acoustics; 2. blackout; 3. CO2-controlled fresh air (small but reliable, and cheap given the Panasonic unit is already specified); 4. room temperature control; 5. bed-level temperature control (plausible mechanism, no RCT found); 6. weighted blanket as an opt-in item (big effect only shown in clinical insomnia; hygiene burden of a 6–8 kg blanket between guests is real); 7. lavender as opt-in (moderate subjective effect; allergy/consent issues); 8. pink/brown masking as default-off, user-selectable (no harm, weak benefit, but it also raises the floor against transient corridor noise — see KQ3).
- The CO2 effect sizes (1–2 % sleep efficiency, 5–8 min awake) are small per night but are the only interventions here with repeated objective polysomnographic/actigraphic replication; they matter most for the 8–12 h stays, far less for 2 h naps.

### Gaps
- No RCT or controlled study on cooling mattress pads (Eight Sleep, Chilipad, BedJet) was found; the search budget ran out before a targeted query. Eight Sleep's own claims should be treated as marketing.
- Eye-mask RCT (e.g. Greco et al. 2023, Sleep, on episodic learning and alertness): not retrieved; search budget exhausted.
- Zhao 2024 pooled effect sizes for weighted blankets: not retrievable.
- Dose/administration details (drops, diffuser type, minutes per night) in the lavender trials: not retrievable from snippets; allergy/contact-dermatitis evidence not searched.
- Mattress firmness evidence (e.g. Caggiari 2021 systematic review on medium-firm mattresses) and bed-microclimate targets (≈32–34 °C under the duvet): not retrieved.

### Inferences
- Thermal load (derived; physics only, assumptions stated): cabin volume 10.6–12.7 m³; envelope area ≈ 29–33 m². Ventilation heat exchange without recovery = 0.34 Wh/(m³·K) × flow × ΔT: at 36 m³/h and 5 K (host building 25 °C, cabin 20 °C) ≈ 60 W; at 180 m³/h ≈ 300 W. Add occupant (a sleeping adult is commonly taken at roughly 70–80 W — not verified here), lighting/electronics (~20–50 W assumed) and conduction through sandwich panels (assume U 0.5–1.0 W/m²K × 30 m² × 5 K = 75–150 W). Design cooling capacity ≈ 300–600 W sensible at low ventilation, up to ~900 W if the ERV runs at full flow without recovery; a heat-recovery core typically removes most of the ventilation term. Heating in a conditioned office is negligible; in an unheated station a 300–500 W heater suffices. A Peltier system would need roughly 2× that in electrical power because of its low COP; a small split or water-chiller loop (as in Chilipad/Eight Sleep hubs) is more efficient but adds a condenser/heat-rejection path into the host building.
- Bed-level cooling (water mattress pad) is the most power-efficient way to deliver the "cool bed" the Baniassadi/bedding evidence supports, because it conditions the microclimate rather than 11 m³ of air; but water hubs need refilling, descaling and the pad must survive a 20-minute reset with an encasement over it.
- Acoustic ventilation: the ERV should run at the lowest speed that holds CO2 < 800 ppm, since fan noise and duct break-in are the likely noise floor inside a quiet cabin; silencers on supply/exhaust are mandatory to preserve the envelope's attenuation.
- Sound masking: given low evidence of benefit but no harm, a single small speaker playing pink/brown noise at a user-selectable level (default off, or on at a low level in stations) is a low-cost hedge that also masks intermittent corridor noise; the review data favour pink over white.

## Key Question 4 — Which hygiene concepts allow a fully automatic, staff-light reset?

### Takeaway
UV-C can disinfect surfaces/air but only in an unoccupied, interlocked cabin, with ozone-free lamp selection (254 nm generates no ozone; 185 nm and 222 nm sources can) and attention to dose; ozone generators and ozone-emitting ionisers should be excluded because of ozone and VOC-reaction by-products. No source was found describing automated linen-cassette exchange or validated antimicrobial textiles for pods, so the 20-minute reset remains a logistics design problem rather than an evidence-backed one.

### Cited Findings
- UV-C over-exposure damages eyes and skin depending on wavelength, intensity, distance and time; hospitality operators are urged to be cautious and to understand "dosage", a term uncommon in the industry — [Hospitality Technology: caution with UVC](https://hospitalitytech.com/why-hotels-restaurants-should-be-cautious-using-uvc-sanitizing-tech)
- Whole-room UV-C systems achieve higher doses and disinfect surfaces and air simultaneously but require space evacuation and cannot run with occupants present; upper-room fixtures must be above eye level with barriers — [ISSA Today, March–April 2024](https://issatoday.mydigitalpublication.com/march-april-2024/page-33)
- UV-C at 254 nm does not generate ozone; ozone is a concern for other lamp types; unmonitored ozone is hazardous and reacts with VOCs to form particulate matter — [Holtop: "ozone-free" lamp safety](https://www.holtop.com/news/is-an-ozone-free-lamp-absolutely-safe-can-people-stay-indoors-during-use)
- Far-UVC 222 nm operated in a standard hotel room (Baltimore) increased indoor ozone, though levels stayed below outdoor and below US EPA ambient limits; a related medRxiv preprint measured ozone and ultrafine particles in a hotel quarantine facility under 222 nm disinfection (preprint, not peer-reviewed) — [medRxiv preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.09.29.23296366v1.full.pdf+html)
- Vendor/industry pieces claim UV-C destroys "up to 99.99 %" of surface and airborne pathogens and describe robots operating autonomously during unoccupied periods; a certification path via an OSHA Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory is mentioned — [Hospitality Net: UV LEDs in hotel rooms](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4076536.html); [Hotel Management: California resort UV-C protocols](https://www.hotelmanagement.net/tech/california-resort-implements-uv-c-disinfection-protocols); [Hospitality Technology: 3 reasons UV-C](https://hospitalitytech.com/3-reasons-why-uv-c-future-disinfection-hotel-industry)
- Ioniser by-products (ozone, ultrafine particles from ozone–limonene reactions, oxygenated VOCs): see KQ2 — [PDXScholar (Alshawa 2007)](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/mengin_fac/344); [science.gov topic page](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/a/air+ionization)
- Zedwell markets "purified air" / filtered air in every room as part of its hygiene-and-sleep proposition (operator claim) — [Business Traveller on Zedwell Knightsbridge](https://www.businesstraveller.com/business-travel/2025/02/25/zedwell-opens-windowless-hotel-in-londons-knightsbridge/)

### Inferences
- A workable automatic cycle: (1) door-lock interlock and occupancy sensor confirm the cabin is empty; (2) ERV boost to full flow for the whole reset (≈15 air changes in 10 min at 180 m³/h for a 12 m³ cabin) to flush odour/VOCs and CO2 — this also removes any residual scent from the previous guest; (3) optional 254 nm UV-C surface pass only while locked and with a lamp-off interlock on door opening; (4) no ozone, no ioniser; (5) HEPA + activated-carbon on the supply side for odour and PM2.5 (WELL A05's 10 µg/m³ target).
- Linen: with no automation evidence found, the pragmatic design is a fitted waterproof/breathable encasement on mattress and pillow (changed on a longer cycle) plus a single-piece fitted sheet + duvet cover "cassette" that one person can swap in under 5 minutes; weighted blankets need their own washable cover and a defined wash cycle.

### Gaps
- No sources on cassette/robotic linen exchange, hotel linen turnaround times, or mattress-encasement/dust-mite evidence were retrieved (search budget exhausted before these queries).
- No evidence on antimicrobial (silver/copper) textiles' real-world efficacy was retrieved.
- Legal limits for ozone and UV-C in German workplaces/public spaces (e.g. TRGS, DIN EN 62471 photobiological safety) were not researched.
- Scent/odour sensing (VOC sensors as "freshness" verification after reset): no sources retrieved.

## Key Question 6 — Small-space psychology (claustrophobia mitigation in a 2 × 2.4 × 2.2 m room) and German minimum-room rules

### Takeaway
No study directly tests claustrophobia in sleep pods, but perception research shows lighter ceilings and walls and wall-washing (cornice) light make rooms look taller, that perceived spaciousness scales with floor area and ceiling height and falls with clutter, and that LED "virtual windows" improve perceived lighting in windowless rooms. Regulatory flag: Berlin's building code (BauO Bln § 47, as quoted in a 2024 parliamentary submission) requires 2.40 m clear height for Aufenthaltsräume and German workplace rules require 2.50 m, so a 2.2 m-high cabin can only work if it is classified as furniture/equipment inside a host room rather than as an Aufenthaltsraum or Beherbergungsraum — this needs legal confirmation.

### Cited Findings
- Oberfeld, Hecht & Gamer 2010 (older): in VR experiments, lighter ceilings make a room look taller and lighter walls contribute, while floor lightness has little effect — [Oberfeld et al. 2010 (PDF)](https://www.staff.uni-mainz.de/oberfeld/downloads/oberfeld_hecht_gamer_2010.pdf); [UKE record: surface lightness influences perceived room height](https://fis.uke.de/portal/en/publications/surface-lightness-influences-perceived-room-height(f39b6164-cc0b-417e-8439-45377a78d502)/export.html)
- Cornice/wall-wash lighting that illuminates the upper wall surfaces increased perceived ceiling height (link-to-finding attribution taken from the search summary; page not opened) — [CMS conference paper on cornice lighting](https://openaccess-api.cms-conferences.org/articles/download/978-1-964867-86-1_22)
- Perceived spaciousness correlates positively with horizontal area and/or ceiling height; more objects make a room feel crowded and cramped (link-to-finding attribution taken from the search summary; page not opened) — [NSF-hosted paper on spaciousness](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10143339)
- LED virtual windows in windowless consultation rooms improved perceived lighting conditions and reduced reported concentration difficulties (measured lighting impression, not claustrophobia) — [DMJ: LED virtual windows in windowless consultation rooms](https://ufl.test1.eksponent.com/dmj/led-virtual-windows-are-valuable-windowless-consultation-rooms)
- In isolated and confined environments (Bilkent study), interior architectural elements had relatively minor impact on spatial perception compared with the evaluator's design training — [Bilkent repository: spaciousness in ICE](https://repository.bilkent.edu.tr/items/135d0da6-5c31-4bb0-9acf-6b67141c58cd)
- Berlin: a 2024 submission to the Berlin parliament quotes § 47 (1) BauO Bln: "Aufenthaltsräume müssen eine lichte Raumhöhe von mindestens 2,40 m haben" (quoted in a lobbying document proposing to delete the clause; verify current text) — [Berlin parliament lobby-register submission (PDF)](https://pardok.parlament-berlin.de/starweb/adis/citat/VT/19/Lobbyregister/Beteiligungsbeitrag_3236_Buendnis%20Junger%20Genossenschaften.pdf)
- Bavaria (comparison): BayBO Art. 45 requires 2.40 m clear height for Aufenthaltsräume (2.20 m over half the floor area in attics), not applying to Gebäudeklassen 1–2 — [BayBO Art. 45](https://www.gesetze-bayern.de/Content/Document/BayBO-45)
- Workplaces: ASR A1.2 (Arbeitsstättenverordnung) requires 2.50 m clear height, cited in a Nuremberg day-care construction guide — [Nürnberg bauliche Anforderungen (PDF)](https://www.nuernberg.de/imperia/md/kinderbetreuung/dokumente/kita_ausbau/bauliche_anforderungen.pdf); [bau.de forum on 2.50 m commercial rooms](https://bau.de/forum/planung/11473.php)
- Hotel room size: an (unofficial) guide to letting holiday accommodation states that some state building codes set minimum sizes, most commonly 8 m² for a single and 12 m² for a double room (excluding bath/WC) — [Hinweise zur Vermietung von Ferienunterkünften (PDF)](https://os-syru3-813963.meinecloud.io/b10290/leer/%C3%9Cbernachten/Hinweise_zur_Vermietung_von_Ferienunterk%C3%BCnften.pdf)
- Hamburg's Beherbergungsstättenverordnung defines Beherbergungsräume as rooms serving guests' stay or sleep (suites count as one room) — [umwelt-online: Hamburg BeVO](https://umwelt-online.de/recht/bau/laender/hh/bevo.htm)

### Inferences
- Design levers that follow from the perception literature: matte light ceiling and upper walls, darker floor, cornice/cove uplight on the upper wall band, zero visible clutter (recessed controls, no loose furniture), a low bed to maximise headroom above eye level, and an optional "virtual window"/sky panel on a dimmable warm channel that is fully dark during sleep. A full-width mirror is a common suggestion but no evidence was retrieved either way.
- At 2.0 × 2.4 m = 4.8 m² and 2.4 × 2.4 m = 5.76 m², the cabin is below the ~8 m² single-room floor commonly cited for hotel rooms and below the 2.40 m Aufenthaltsraum height; the viable regulatory path is likely "nap/rest furniture within an existing permitted use" for short stays, whereas 12-hour overnight stays and more than a handful of beds per site may trigger Beherbergungsstätten (special-building) rules. This is a critical go/no-go item for the Berlin pilot.

### Gaps
- Berlin's own Beherbergungsstätten rules (Betriebs-Verordnung Berlin; bed-count threshold for Sonderbau status) were not found in the retrieved results.
- No study on claustrophobia or anxiety specifically in capsule hotels/sleep pods was retrieved; the Nine Hours/napcabs guest-experience literature would be the nearest proxy and was not reached.
- Evidence on mirrors, colour palettes and material choices for sleep (beyond the lightness findings above) was not retrieved.
