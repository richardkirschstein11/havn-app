# HAVN cabin add-on services: what comparable operators sell, at what price, and how well it sells

Method note: all findings below come from web search result summaries (the session's outbound proxy returned 403/ENOTFOUND for every direct page fetch, including oaky.com, napcabs.com, ninehours.co.jp, minutesuites.com, hoteltechreport.com, hospitalitynet.org). Prices are therefore as reported by the cited page at the date noted; where a source is pre-2021 it is flagged "(old)". Currency conversions are not applied; EUR is used where the source quotes EUR.

## Key question 1: Inventory table of add-ons that real operators sell (operator, add-on, price, format, attach rate/revenue share)

### Takeaway
Across sleep pods, capsule hotels, airport lounges, recovery studios and sleep hotels, the add-ons that are actually priced and sold fall into seven recurring buckets: (1) time extensions per hour, (2) shower access, (3) bedding/comfort items (pillow, blanket, towel, loungewear), (4) luggage storage/lockers, (5) late check-out / early check-in, (6) memberships that bundle a discount plus guaranteed availability, and (7) sleep-tracking/sleep-report upgrades. Published attach rates are rare; the only hard data are from hotel upsell platforms (1.6 % to 23 % conversion depending on the offer and channel) and minibars (25-35 % of guests use them).

### Inferences
- The price anchors most relevant to HAVN's Berlin pilot are Napcabs (EUR 12-17/h, EUR 34 minimum), YOTELAIR Schiphol (EUR 95-125 per 4 h, EUR 10/h extension), Lufthansa paid lounge access (EUR 25-49) and citizenM's EUR 29 late check-out. HAVN's hourly extension pricing should sit between Napcabs' night rate and YOTELAIR's EUR 10/h extension to be read as "fair" by German travellers.
- Showers are sold separately at EUR/USD 7-30 per use by Nine Hours (JPY 700-1,000), Minute Suites (USD 20-30) and Plaza Premium (SGD 16 in 2015), and are discounted when bundled with a cabin (Minute Suites USD 30 -> USD 20). ## Key question 2: Which add-ons are standard, which are differentiators, and which have been dropped or failed (and why)?

### Takeaway
Standard across operators: hourly extensions, shower access, lockers/luggage storage, free Wi-Fi, included bedding and basic toiletries, and a lounge-programme tie-in (Priority Pass). Differentiators: sleep-data reports (Nine Hours), recovery treatments bundled into sleep packages (Equinox Hotel), pillow menus and sleep concierges (Benjamin, Conrad), and membership perks that guarantee availability (citizenM). Documented failures are mostly whole formats rather than add-ons: China's shared sleep capsules (shut down for fire safety, disposable bedding disliked), Nap York (closed, reason not published), Sleepbox Dulles (listed temporarily closed), and hotel minibars in general (70 %+ of guests never use them, revenue 0.24 % of hotel revenue).

### Inferences
- HAVN's Rest kit (eye mask, earplugs, tea) has no direct precedent as a paid SKU among pod operators; the closest analogues are hotel "sleep kits" sold inside packages (The Benjamin) and trade kits bought wholesale at GBP 32.99 per box. Pod operators include these items or do not offer them; the Zedwell guest who brought her own earplugs suggests latent demand at sleep-positioned properties.
- The most reliable paid extras, by evidence weight, are time-based (extensions, late check-out, early check-in) and shower access. Physical consumables (minibar-type) have the weakest documented economics unless automated and price-reasonable.
- Format-level failures (China, Nap York) were driven by regulation/fire safety and, probably, by low price points (USD 10 per 30 min; CNY 6-10 per 30 min) rather than by add-on design; HAVN's room-in-room cabins with a sanitary core are closer to Napcabs/YOTELAIR, which persist.

### Gaps
- No source explains why Nap York closed or whether Sleepbox's closure is permanent; no reporting found on whether Nap York's meals/classes or Sleepbox's extras contributed revenue.

## Key question 3: What do unattended operators do about hygiene, restocking and theft for extras?

### Takeaway
Unattended and lightly staffed rest operators handle hygiene by cleaning after every use (Napcabs), issuing fresh textiles at check-in (Nine Hours), charging for pillow/blanket at the bare-pod end (GoSleep) or using disposable bedding and UV sanitising (China); theft is managed with lockable safety boxes and lockers rather than lockable cabins (First Cabin, Japanese capsule norms); unattended retail relies on weight-sensor fridges and once-daily restocking, and budgets 4-6 % shrink.

### Cited Findings

## Key question 4: What attach rates and revenue per guest do upsell platforms and minibar studies report, and which categories convert best?

### Takeaway
Independent-ish data (Akia, 4,729 upsells to July 2026) show a 1.59 % overall attach rate with early check-in and late check-out taking 90 % of revenue at a modal USD 50; vendor case studies (Oaky) show 10-23 % conversion on pre-arrival offers and EUR 50-147 incremental revenue per converting request/room; breakfast is the largest single category on Oaky (29 % of ancillary revenue). Minibars reach 25-35 % of guests but yield only USD 0.60-2.45 per occupied room; room service tops out at EUR 257 per room per year in the best-quartile European hotels.

### Cited Findings

- Akia (12 months to July 2026, ~24 hotel and vacation-rental operators, 4,729 paid upsells worth USD 370,625): early check-in + late check-out = 90 % of revenue and 96 % of sales; early check-in alone 58 % of revenue; most common price USD 50, then USD 100; price tracked room rate (USD 20 at a budget motel to USD 250+ at a luxury boutique); 58 % of purchases between 07:00 and 13:00 local time (early check-in peaks about 10:00, late check-out about 08:00); overall attach rate 1.59 %; no measurable review-score lift from upsell buyers — [Akia](https://www.akia.com/blog/which-hotel-upsells-sell-best); [Akia timing study](https://www.akia.com/blog/early-check-in-and-late-checkout-when-guests-actually-buy-them)

### Inferences
- For a rest station, the hotel analogues of HAVN's best-converting products are time-based: hourly extensions (= late check-out), early access (= early check-in) and priority holds. Akia's modal USD 50 price and 1.6 % attach rate on hotels imply that at HAVN's shorter stays an extension priced at EUR 10-17/h (Napcabs/YOTELAIR levels) will likely convert far higher than physical kits.
